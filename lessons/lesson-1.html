Financial Literacy · Lesson 1

What is financial literacy?

About 20 minutes · 6 activities · Your typed answers are saved in this browser

Every day you make money decisions, even if you don't notice them. Buying lunch or packing one. Spending birthday money right away or keeping some. Signing up for a "free" trial that turns into a monthly charge. Each choice is small, but together they shape how much freedom you'll have later.

This lesson explains what financial literacy really means, why it matters more for you now than it seems, and the five building blocks that everything else in this course is built on. Along the way you'll type, match, and calculate, because you remember what you do, not just what you read.

Breaking down the words

The term has two parts, and each one tells you something.

  • Financial means anything to do with money: earning it, spending it, saving it, growing it, and borrowing it.
  • Literacy usually means being able to read and write. More broadly, it means being able to understand something well enough to use it. Someone who is "computer literate" can actually get things done on a computer, not just turn it on.

Put them together and financial literacy is the ability to "read" money situations: to understand what's really going on with a paycheck, a price tag, a loan offer, or an investment, and to make a good choice because of it.

Financial literacy (noun)

Having the knowledge, skills and confidence to make informed decisions about money, so that money supports your goals instead of controlling your life.

Notice the three parts of that definition. They're different, and you need all three:

  • Knowledge is knowing the facts: what interest is, what a budget is, what "net pay" means.
  • Skills are being able to use those facts: comparing two prices per ounce, building a budget, spotting a bad deal.
  • Confidence is actually acting on what you know: asking questions before you sign something, saying no to a purchase, starting to save even when the amount feels small.

Someone can know that saving is important and still never do it. Financial literacy isn't a test score. It shows up in what you actually do with money.

What financial literacy is not

A few common myths get in the way:

  • "It's about being rich." No. People with high incomes can still end up broke if they spend everything they make. People with modest incomes can build real security with good habits. It's about how you handle money, not how much you have.
  • "It's just about stocks." Investing is one part. Most of financial literacy is everyday stuff: budgeting, avoiding fees, understanding a paycheck, borrowing carefully.
  • "I'll learn it when I'm older." Money habits form early, and time is the most powerful tool you have. Starting at 15 instead of 30 can make a difference worth tens of thousands of dollars, as you'll see below.

The five building blocks

Almost every money topic fits into one of five building blocks. The rest of this course covers each one in detail, so here's what each means and the key words that come with it.

1 Earning

Earning is how money comes in: a part-time job, babysitting, an allowance, selling things you make. Money coming in is called income.

An important surprise for first-time workers: the amount on your job offer isn't what lands in your account. Your gross pay is what you earn before anything is taken out. Your net pay (also called take-home pay) is what's left after taxes and other deductions. If you're offered $15 an hour, you'll take home somewhat less than $15 for every hour you work. Budgets are always built on net pay, because that's the money you can actually use.

2 Spending

Money going out is called an expense. Spending well starts with telling needs (things you must pay for, like food and getting to school) from wants (things that are nice but optional).

Every purchase also has a hidden price called opportunity cost: whatever you give up by choosing it. If you spend $60 on a game, the opportunity cost might be the concert ticket you can no longer afford, or $60 that could have been growing in savings. Thinking "what am I giving up?" before buying is one of the most useful money habits there is.

3 Saving

Saving means setting money aside for later instead of spending it now. A budget (a plan for your money before you spend it) makes saving happen on purpose instead of by accident.

Two saving ideas matter most. First, pay yourself first: move money into savings as soon as you get it, before spending on wants. Second, build an emergency fund, money kept aside for surprises like a cracked phone screen or a lost bus pass, so a surprise doesn't become a crisis.

4 Investing

Investing means putting money into something that can grow over time, like shares of a company or a fund that holds many companies. It's different from saving: savings are safe and easy to reach, while investments can grow more but can also lose value. That trade-off is called risk. In general, a chance of higher reward comes with more risk.

The engine behind investing is compound growth (or compound interest): you earn returns not only on the money you put in, but also on the returns you already earned. Growth builds on growth, which is why starting early matters so much. Try it yourself in the calculator below.

5 Borrowing

Borrowing means using someone else's money now and paying it back later, usually with interest, an extra cost for borrowing. A credit card, a car loan and a student loan are all borrowing. Interest works for you when you save and against you when you borrow.

Lenders decide whether to lend to you, and how much to charge, partly based on your credit score, a number that reflects how reliably you've paid back money in the past. Borrowing carefully and always paying on time builds a strong score, which makes future borrowing (like a first apartment or car) cheaper.

The big picture Earning brings money in. Spending and saving decide where it goes. Investing helps it grow. Borrowing lets you use money you don't have yet, at a price. Financial literacy is understanding how all five connect.

Why this matters more than it seems

The calculator shows the most important idea in this lesson: time is your biggest advantage. Someone who saves $20 a month from age 15 can end up with far more than someone who saves $40 a month from age 35, even though the second person puts in more money. Habits you build now, with small amounts, are the same habits that will run your finances when the amounts are much bigger.

Financial literacy also protects you. People who understand interest are harder to trick with "buy now, pay later" offers. People who read the fine print notice a free trial that becomes a $15 monthly charge. People with an emergency fund don't have to borrow at high interest when something breaks. Every one of these is a skill, and every skill can be learned.
