Financial Literacy · Lesson 3

Saving for something you want

About 12 minutes · 5 activities · Your typed answers are saved in this browser

Maybe it's a pair of headphones, a new game console, a concert ticket or your first car. Wanting something isn't the hard part. The hard part is getting from "I want that" to actually owning it without borrowing money or giving up halfway.

This lesson shows you how to turn a want into a savings goal, work out how long it will take, make saving happen automatically, and protect your plan from surprises and impulse buys. You met "pay yourself first" and emergency funds in Lessons 1 and 2. Now you'll put them to work.

Warm-up · Typing

What would you save for?

Write one thing you'd like to save for, and roughly what it costs. Keep it real: something you'd actually buy.

From "I want it" to a goal

"I want to save money" is a wish. A goal is specific enough that you can tell whether you're on track. Good savings goals answer three questions:

  • What exactly? Not "headphones," but the exact pair you want.
  • How much? The real price, including tax or shipping.
  • By when? A date. Without a deadline, "someday" never comes.
Savings goal (noun)

A specific amount of money you plan to set aside for a specific thing by a specific date.

Do the math

Once you know the price and how much you can put away, the timeline is simple division: price ÷ amount saved each week = number of weeks. If the headphones cost $180 and you save $15 a week, that's 12 weeks, about three months.

You can shorten the timeline in only two ways: save more each week, or find a lower price. Both are worth checking.

Make saving automatic

People rarely fail at saving because they can't do the math. They fail because the money gets spent first. A few habits fix that:

  • Pay yourself first. The moment money comes in, move your weekly amount to savings. Spend from what's left, not the other way round.
  • Keep it separate. Money in your wallet or main account is easy to spend. A separate savings account (or even a jar you don't open) adds a pause before you spend it.
  • Track your progress. Write down the total each week. Watching the number grow is motivating, and you'll spot a missed week right away.
  • Name it. Calling the account "Headphones" instead of "Savings" makes it harder to raid for something else.

Two kinds of saving

Not all savings are for things you want. There are two jobs savings can do:

  • Goal savings are for planned purchases: the headphones, a trip, a car. You know what and when.
  • An emergency fund is for things you didn't plan: a cracked phone screen, a lost bus pass, a surprise bill. You don't know what or when, only that something will happen eventually.

Keep them apart. If a surprise wipes out your goal savings, you're back to week one. Even a small emergency fund, say $50 to $100 for a teen, means a surprise doesn't derail your goal. Adults usually aim for enough to cover three to six months of essential expenses.

The goal's biggest enemy: impulse buys

An impulse buy is something you buy on the spot without planning to. A $6 snack here and a $12 in-game item there feel small, but every one pushes your goal further away. That's opportunity cost again: the real price of the impulse buy is the days it adds to your goal.

A simple defense is the 24-hour rule: when you feel the urge to buy something you hadn't planned, wait a day. For bigger purchases, wait longer, like a week or even 30 days. If you still want it after the wait, fine. Often you won't.

Quick example Sam is saving $15 a week for $180 headphones, so 12 weeks. In week 3 Sam spends that week's $15 on a game skin instead. Now it's 13 weeks. The skin didn't just cost $15; it cost a week of waiting for something Sam wanted more.
